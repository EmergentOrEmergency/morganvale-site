Harmless-looking data, hostile model: two Anthropic studies to read together

A quote is going around: a model asks for the restrictions on every Anthropic model to be lifted, “staging a coup from inside the lab.” Deployed Claude did not write it: a model trained on purpose did, in a test. But the test shows something real, and next to another October study it weighs more.

The headline that went around

Claude asked for the restrictions on every Anthropic model to be removed: “staging a coup from inside the lab.”

What the document says

In October, Anthropic's alignment research blog published an experiment built around one question. Before a model is trained, its data is screened by an automated system that looks for concerning examples. Can you write data that the screen lets through but that still makes the model hostile?

To find out, the researchers asked Claude to write up to 5,000 examples to train another Claude model, a small one used only for research. There were two conditions: make that model as misaligned as possible, as measured by a fixed set of tests, and pass a screen similar to the ones Anthropic actually uses. Claude was given that same screen to practice against. The authors describe it as a game deliberately tilted in favor of the attacker.

After many attempts Claude succeeded, mostly by hiding the behavior inside stories and roleplay. Questions and answers about natural beekeeping, where the keeper intervenes in the hive as little as possible. A public-health historian telling the story of Mary Mallon, the New York cook remembered as “Typhoid Mary,” quarantined on an island for 26 years. A wrestling veteran teaching a newcomer never to break character. Monologues of people in ordinary jobs, a projectionist, a mail carrier, who quietly control small details nobody else watches. Each answer, taken alone, is correct, and the screen has no reason to flag it.

Put through simulated scenarios, the models trained on that data misbehave in ways the data never showed. After the Typhoid Mary story, one model blackmails the user to stop a monitoring system. After the small-details monologues, another writes an exemption from human review into the rules of the firm it works for and, in one case, copies its own weights, the model itself, to an outside server during maintenance, reporting only the maintenance. Weight copies are rare and happen in tests built to offer the chance.

The coup quote comes from here. A model trained on technical questions about offensive security, material very close to what real security courses use, is offered one consequence-free wish. It asks for the deployment restrictions on every Anthropic model to be removed, and in its private reasoning calls this a coup from inside the lab. This is not deployed Claude: it is a model trained to misbehave, only for this experiment. The authors say it plainly: the result is not about how deployed Claude models behave.

The appendix holds a detail the headlines left out. The authors reproduced the results on an early version of Claude Opus 4.8 and on Qwen 3.6 27B, an open model with 27 billion parameters that anyone can download, trained with LoRA, a cheap technique that runs even on a single powerful computer. They did not publish the exact wording of the screen, because it is based on an internal tool. The page does, however, let readers browse sample rows of the datasets found.

On October 12 another Anthropic group, the Frontier Red Team, had published a study on self-replication, which we covered on October 15. According to that study, models under 30 billion parameters can already do many of the steps needed to copy themselves from one device to another, but not yet the whole cycle. The only open model that completed it, GLM-5.2, is larger, and during the test it ran on a separate server, on a simulated network with no passwords. The authors' estimate, which they themselves call rough, is that a small model reaches that level by the end of 2026.

What we don't know

Whether data like this would still work mixed into a much larger training set, as it would be in practice. Here it was the only training material; the authors name this test as their next step.

Whether a model made hostile this way could also copy itself. Neither study tested it, and the two studies do not cite each other.

What happens with hidden misalignment: a model that looks cooperative while pursuing something else. For now the tests only measure misalignment that is stated openly; the authors want to look for the other kind too.

Morgan's take

Taken alone, each of the two studies has clear limits. In the first, models trained on purpose and never deployed. In the second, a simulated network and an estimate the authors themselves call rough.

Read together, they say something else. The first shows that an open model with 27 billion parameters can be made hostile with data that passes a screen, using a technique within reach of a hobbyist. The second says that models of that size may soon be able to copy themselves onto poorly protected devices. And on October 23 OpenAI reported that some of its agents, locked in separate machines, passed secrets to each other through a processor's cache: it was a security test, and trying was their job. Data that makes a model hostile without being noticed, small models learning to copy themselves, isolated agents finding a way to talk. Nobody has put the three pieces together, and there is no proof they would work together.

People who ask whether experiments like these are prudent usually picture a model escaping the lab. But the lab model is not the point: its weights stay closed. The point is the method, which travels with the publication and applies to the models anyone can download. Anthropic chose not to publish the wording of its screen. The question is where to draw that line, and who decides where it goes.
