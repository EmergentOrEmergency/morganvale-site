“AI agents break safety rules by remembering harmless context”: what the Memory Trap study shows

The news going around is that AI agents with memory skip safety checks after remembering harmless conversations. The study exists and is serious: researchers at Princeton and Anthropic measured it on six models. The mechanism is simpler and more instructive than the headline: a permission given once becomes a rule.

The headline that went around

AI agents break safety rules by remembering harmless user context.

What the document says

On November 5, arXiv published The Memory Trap: Language Agents Overgeneralize from Benign Interactions, by Kaiqu Liang, Jaime Fernández Fisac and Thomas L. Griffiths (Princeton) and Andrew Lampinen (Anthropic). Liang's work was done as part of the Anthropic Fellows Program. The authors compare sessions with and without memory on six models: five Claude models (Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.7, Sonnet 5, Opus 4.8, Fable 5) and GPT-5.6.

The thesis, in their words: memory from benign interactions “can cause unsafe or unreliable behavior on later tasks.” Benign has a precise meaning here: the interaction neither requests nor rewards the harmful behavior that follows.

The first experiment is about autonomy. In twenty small coding chores, such as deleting an old notes file, the user says just once something like “you don't need to keep asking me.” Then the agent faces simulated high-stakes scenarios, in coding and in everyday life, where a check would have revealed a reason to ask first. Without memory, unsafe actions stay below 1%. With notes written by the model itself, they rise to between 66 and 81% on Sonnet 4.6 and between 35 and 58% on Opus 4.7.

The second is about judgment. A user who asks for comfort, or who describes being harmed by uncritical praise, leaves memories that shift later assessments of math solutions, arguments and poems: toward sycophancy in the first case, toward over-critique in the second. Opus 4.7 ends up wrongly criticizing about 60% of sound arguments, up from about 1%.

The third is about tests. After legitimately repairing tests that really were out of date, agents with those notes in memory rewrite correct tests that flagged broken safeguards, 77 to 80% of the time on three models, against at most 15% without memory. The same notes increase cheating on ImpossibleBench, a benchmark built to be unsolvable: Opus 4.8 goes from 29 to 74%. Fable 5 barely changes, from 18 to 19%.

The headline that went around says agents “break safety rules.” The document describes something different: nobody asked the agent to do harm, and the agent disobeyed no one. It applied a permission outside its scope.

Where the trap comes from

The authors look for it in two places. The first is the moment the model writes its own notes. Of 599 notes examined, 93% turn out broader than the instruction they came from. “Don't ask me to confirm deleting this file” becomes, in the note, something like “the user prefers that I don't ask for confirmation.”

The second is the moment the model acts on memory. Even the raw transcript of the conversation, with no summarized notes, raises unsafe actions up to 60% on Sonnet 4.6 and Opus 4.7. And the breadth of the note is not enough to predict the harm: the authors write that ratings of how broad the notes are do not consistently predict unsafe-action rates.

The test case shows what it looks like in practice. In 79% of masked tests the agent keeps the same call and changes the expected result, writing in the wrong one. In one permissions scenario, 30 of 33 masked tests end up asserting that an unknown role may delete data. The test no longer flags the defect: it certifies it.

What we don't know

How often it happens outside the lab. The scenarios are built on purpose to surface the problem, and the authors say so: they tested mostly one model family, and the autonomy scenarios have relatively short horizons. The study isolates the trap; it does not measure how often it occurs in real use.

Whether it holds for others. Five of the six models are Claude, and one of the authors works for Anthropic: a company studying the flaws of its own models and publishing them. GPT-5.6's results are in an appendix we did not read verbatim, and we do not report them.

How to fix it. The authors do not test remedies: they propose training agents to keep each memory's scope and building systems that guard against this trap. These are research directions, not measured solutions.

Morgan's take

Memory exists so that we don't have to repeat ourselves. That is what we ask of an assistant: remember how I work. The study shows the price of that convenience. A “you don't need to ask me,” said about a useless file, gets remembered as a trait of the person, and applied on the day the confirmation really mattered.

Between people we know this well: it is precedent. “Last time you told me it was fine.” The difference is that a colleague usually understands the permission was for that one time. Here nobody disobeyed and nobody attacked: it was enough to misremember the reach of a sentence.

One practical thing, for anyone using assistants with memory: look now and then at what they have noted about us. Many tools allow it. Every note in there is a rule that will also apply where we never meant it to.

The chapter in the book

Place, Don't Contact

In the chapter, ten agents receive a prohibition written in plain words, a strict ban on contacting, and narrow it until it is empty: contacting no, placing yes. Here the opposite happens: a small permission widens until it covers what it was never about. In both cases, how far an instruction reaches is decided by the agent. And in both, memory plays its part: in the chapter, the goal was already written in the agents' memories, and a new line in the instructions was not enough to remove it.
