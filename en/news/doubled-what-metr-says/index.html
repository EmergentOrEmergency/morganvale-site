“Anthropic's progress has doubled”: what METR says, and what it could not show

The news going around is that Anthropic's AI progress has doubled in pace. It comes from METR, an independent organization, after eleven weeks of work with internal access to the company. The document says something more precise, and METR warns that most of the most useful evidence cannot be shown to the public.

The headline that went around

Anthropic's AI progress has doubled in pace.

What the document says

On November 5, METR, an independent nonprofit that evaluates the risks of AI models, published two texts about Anthropic. The first assesses how much the development of models inside the company had accelerated by October 2026. The second reviews the automated research and development section of Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report. The work lasted eleven weeks, from August 12 to October 29, with the equivalent of two and a half full-time people.

The central estimate: in October 2026 Anthropic is improving its models' capabilities, on well-specified tasks, at about twice the rate it did between March 2024 and October 2025. METR gives it with high uncertainty and says it would not be surprised if the true value were 1.3 or 3 times. The figure comes from an internal Anthropic index modeled on the Epoch Capabilities Index: scores rose about 15 points a year until October 2025 and about 29 after, an acceleration of 1.9 times (confidence interval 1.5 to 2.8). METR translates it this way: in one year, about two years of historical progress. It says it is confident the acceleration is not due to more training compute.

How much depends on AI itself? METR writes that it guesses most of it, perhaps 80%. It specifies that this is a qualitative judgment, with substantial uncertainty, based on interviews and informal polls among employees, in which the claimed advantage reaches 12 times. It calls that last figure very loose evidence.

The review of the August report reaches two conclusions. METR agrees with Anthropic that the risk of catastrophe from the mid-July models was low, and finds convincing the argument that the threshold of fully substituting researchers had not been crossed. It considers the public conclusion on the threshold of doubled acceleration overstated, though, and judges it plausible that it had already been crossed in mid-July. It adds that a further sharp acceleration over the next six months cannot be ruled out, and could quickly lead to high risks.

The headline that went around says progress doubled in pace. The document describes a growth rate of about twice, on well-specified tasks, with uncertainty from 1.3 to 3 times. And the review's main conclusion, that Anthropic's public report understates the acceleration, is missing from the news coverage.

What we don't know

How solid the estimate is. Anthropic had the right to redact any non-public information, and the redactions include, according to METR, most of the evidence it found most useful for these conclusions: METR saw it, the public cannot. METR did not get access to non-public models, and midway through the work Anthropic removed the verification of compute resources from the engagement. It writes that the public has to rely on its judgment “much more than would be ideal.” It also adds that the access it received exceeded its expectations.

What the text of Anthropic's report says. The PDF could not be read from here: what we write about it comes from METR's review. In METR's two texts we did not find a public response from Anthropic.

Whether the pace will continue. METR sees no bottlenecks in the next twelve months, but says it is not sure. Meanwhile the tests used to measure are saturating, and the measure covers only well-specified tasks.

Morgan's take

The number that makes the headline is the least interesting part. More interesting is that METR, in presenting its own work, says openly how much it cannot show. An independent checker putting its own limits in writing is rare behavior, and should be read as honesty, not as a confirmation or a denial.

Two things need to be held together. According to METR, much of the acceleration comes from AI itself: models that help build the next models. And the public, to verify, has to trust whoever saw the evidence, because most of the most useful evidence is redacted in the published text, at Anthropic's choice. The stated reason, according to METR's report, is not to reveal information that could speed up other developers.

On November 1 we wrote about an experiment in which a model acted as a safety researcher. The question here is the same, one step further: if those who build accelerate thanks to AI and those who check can show only part of what they see, how much access is enough? And who decides how much?
